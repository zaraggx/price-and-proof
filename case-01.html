CASE 01 / AIRLINES

Why does your flight cost more than mine?

The same route can have very different fares depending on when the ticket is bought. That looks strange until you think about what an airline is actually selling.

THE SHORT VERSION

A plane has a fixed number of seats. Once the flight leaves, an empty seat is worth nothing to the airline. The firm therefore has to decide not only what price to charge, but when to sell each seat.

1 / START WITH THE MARKET

A seat is a perishable product.

If a supermarket has a tin left tomorrow, it can still sell the tin tomorrow. An airline cannot do that with a seat on today's flight. The opportunity disappears when the plane departs.

This makes airline pricing different from many ordinary markets. The airline has limited capacity and uncertain future demand. Selling a seat today also means giving up the chance to sell it later to someone who might be willing to pay more.

2 / A SIMPLE MODEL

Suppose there are two types of customer.

Leisure traveller

Usually books further ahead and may have several possible dates. A higher fare is more likely to change the decision to travel.

Business traveller

May need to travel on a particular day. Changing the date is harder, so the willingness to pay can be higher.

The distinction is not absolute. People do not come with labels saying “business traveller” or “leisure traveller”. It is a useful model of differences in willingness to pay.

4 / WHAT THE RESEARCH SAYS

It is not simply “airlines raise prices because the flight is nearly full”.

Research using flight-level data finds several forces operating at the same time. Remaining capacity matters, but so do expectations about future demand and differences in passengers' willingness to pay.

Escobari, Rupp and Meskey found substantial within-day price variation. After controlling for inventory, time to departure and demand expectations, they identified a component consistent with dynamic price discrimination. Their data also showed higher prices during office hours and lower prices in the evening, a pattern consistent with differences between business and leisure travellers.

Other research reaches a related but slightly different conclusion. Williams' model finds that dynamic pricing can benefit early-arriving, price-sensitive leisure travellers while charging higher prices to later-arriving business travellers. In his model, total welfare can be higher than under a uniform-price system.

5 / PRICE DISCRIMINATION

Different prices do not automatically mean discrimination.

Price discrimination means charging different prices to different customers for the same product when the difference is related to their willingness to pay rather than simply a difference in cost.

Airline pricing can contain an element of this. But a fare changing because the remaining number of seats has changed is not, by itself, proof of price discrimination.

This distinction matters. A good economic explanation should separate what the data actually show from the story we think might explain it.

THE VERDICT

The price is a decision about time as well as demand.

Airlines are choosing how to allocate a fixed number of seats across customers who arrive at different times and have different willingness to pay. Dynamic pricing gives the airline a way to respond as information about demand changes.

So the question “why did my ticket get more expensive?” has no single answer. Scarcity, expected demand, customer type, competition and the time remaining before departure can all matter.

WHAT THIS CASE DOES NOT PROVE

  • It does not prove that an airline changes prices because it knows who you are.
  • It does not mean prices always rise as departure gets closer.
  • It does not mean dynamic pricing always makes consumers worse off.
  • It does not tell us the exact reason for any one fare without data on that flight and its market.
